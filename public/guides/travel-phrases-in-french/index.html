French for Travel

Travel Phrases in French: Practical French for Travelers

You don’t need to speak fluent French to make travel easier. Learning a small group of practical phrases before your trip can help you greet people politely, order at a café, ask for directions, check into your hotel, and handle everyday situations with more confidence.

Whether you’re visiting France or another French-speaking destination, start with phrases you’re actually likely to use. These essential French travel phrases cover some of the most common situations travelers encounter.

TravelLingo lets you practice these phrases before your trip with pronunciation audio and interactive activities, so they feel more familiar when you need them.

Why learn French before you travel?

Even a small amount of French can make everyday travel interactions easier.

You do not need to hold a long conversation. A few well-practiced phrases can help you greet someone politely, ask a basic question, order food, understand directions, or explain what you need.

The goal is not fluency before your trip. It is familiarity.

If you already recognize the phrases you are most likely to hear and use, you are less likely to freeze when you need them.

Start with polite basics

Bonjour.

Good morning.

Bonsoir.

Good evening.

S’il vous plaît.

Please.

Merci.

Thank you.

Merci beaucoup.

Thank you very much.

Excusez-moi.

Excuse me.

Au revoir.

Goodbye.

In France and many other French-speaking places, greeting someone before immediately asking a question is an important part of a polite interaction.

Instead of walking into a shop and beginning with a request, start with:

Bonjour.

Good morning.

Then ask your question.

Using s’il vous plaît and merci frequently and saying au revoir when leaving a shop, café, or small business can also make everyday interactions feel more natural.

French for restaurants and cafés

Le menu, s’il vous plaît.

The menu, please.

Je voudrais de l’eau.

I would like water.

Je voudrais un café.

I would like coffee.

Une table pour deux, s’il vous plaît.

A table for two, please.

L’addition, s’il vous plaît.

The check, please.

Merci, c’était délicieux.

Thank you, it was delicious.

Je suis végétarien/végétarienne.

I am vegetarian.

Je ne peux pas manger ça.

I cannot eat this.

TravelLingo also teaches useful individual words including eau for water, café for coffee, bière for beer, and vin for wine.

Je voudrais…

I would like…

Je voudrais… is a useful phrase pattern because it gives you a polite way to make a simple request.

For example:

Je voudrais un café.

I would like coffee.

Finding your way

Où est… ?

Where is…?

Où sont les toilettes ?

Where is the bathroom?

Où est l’hôtel ?

Where is the hotel?

Comment puis-je y aller ?

How do I get there?

Est-ce loin ?

Is it far?

Je me suis perdu.

I got lost.

Je ne sais pas où je suis.

I don’t know where I am.

It also helps to recognize the direction words someone may use when answering you.

Gauche.

Left.

Droite.

Right.

Tout droit.

Straight ahead.

Près.

Near.

Loin.

Far.

Rue.

Street.

Learning both the questions you may need to ask and the basic direction words you may hear in response makes this vocabulary much more useful.

Train.

Bus.

At your hotel

J’ai une réservation.

I have a booking.

Puis-je m’enregistrer ?

Can I check in?

À quelle heure est le départ ?

What time is check-out?

Où est l’entrée ?

Where is the entrance?

These phrases are useful for both traditional hotels and rentals, especially when you need an entrance code, Wi-Fi password, towels, or help with your room key.

Shopping in French

Combien ça coûte ?

How much does this cost?

C’est trop cher.

That is too expensive.

Je regarde seulement, merci.

I am just looking, thank you.

Je voudrais acheter ceci.

I would like to buy this.

Avez-vous ceci dans une autre taille ?

Do you have this in another size?

Avez-vous ceci dans une autre couleur ?

Do you have this in another color?

Puis-je l’essayer ?

Can I try it on?

Puis-je payer par carte ?

Can I pay with a card?

Puis-je payer en espèces ?

Can I pay with cash?

Où est la cabine d’essayage ?

Where is the fitting room?

When you do not understand

Je ne comprends pas.

I don’t understand.

Parlez lentement, s’il vous plaît.

Please speak slowly.

Parlez-vous anglais ?

Do you speak English?

Pouvez-vous répéter, s’il vous plaît ?

Can you repeat that?

Pouvez-vous l’écrire, s’il vous plaît ?

Could you write that down, please?

Qu’est-ce que ça veut dire ?

What does that mean?

These may be some of the most useful phrases in the entire guide because they help you keep an interaction going even when your French runs out.

You do not need to understand every word. Sometimes the most useful thing you can say is simply that you did not understand.

Help.

Hopefully you will not need these phrases, but they are worth recognizing before you travel.

Practice French pronunciation before your trip

French spelling can look unfamiliar if you are learning from written phrases alone.

That is why it helps to listen to the pronunciation and say the phrases aloud before your trip.

You do not need a perfect accent. The goal is to become comfortable enough with the sound and rhythm of the phrase that you can recognize it and say it when you need it.

Focus on the phrases that matter most for your own trip, listen to them several times, and practice speaking them aloud.

Build a phrase list around your own trip

You probably do not need every phrase in this guide equally.

Think about the situations you are actually likely to encounter.

If you are staying in a hotel, prioritize check-in, Wi-Fi, and room-related phrases.

If you plan to use trains, learn how to ask about the station, tickets, departure times, and arrival times.

If restaurants are a big part of your trip, focus on ordering, asking for the bill, and communicating dietary needs.

A smaller list of phrases you have practiced several times is often more useful than a much longer list you barely recognize.

French changes from place to place

French is spoken in destinations well beyond France.

Words, accents, and customs can vary between France, Canada, Belgium, Switzerland, and other French-speaking places.

That is a useful reminder when preparing for a trip: learn practical standard French, but expect to hear regional differences once you arrive.

The goal is not to understand every variation. It is to have enough practical French to communicate when it matters.

A simple way to prepare

Reading a phrase once is different from being able to remember it when someone is standing in front of you.

Before your trip, focus on a smaller number of useful phrases and practice them repeatedly.

  1. Start with greetings and polite phrases.
  2. Learn phrases for the situations you expect to encounter most.
  3. Listen to the pronunciation rather than relying only on written French.
  4. Practice saying phrases aloud.
  5. Review them several times before departure.
